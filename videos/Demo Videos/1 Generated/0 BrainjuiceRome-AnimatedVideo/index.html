How the Roman Military Destroyed Rome's Legitimacy
The greatest threat
to the Roman
Empire was not
a foreign invader,
but a single
decision made in
Mainz.
For two hundred
and fifty years,
the Pax Romana
held because the
Roman throne stayed
tied to established
rules.
Succession was messy,
but it always
required the stamp
of dynastic lineage
or Senate approval.
Then came the
year 235,
and a young
emperor named Severus
Alexander faced a
crisis on the
northern border.
Rather than fight
the Germanic tribes,
the young emperor
chose to pay
them for peace.
To his battle-hardened
soldiers, this diplomacy
looked like cowardice.
They felt insulted.
They did not
just complain.
They marched into
the emperor's tent
and murdered him.
With the emperor
dead, the soldiers
bypassed the Senate
entirely to declare
their own ruler.
Maximinus Thrax was
a peasant-born soldier
who had never
set foot inside
the Senate.
This single act
shattered the fragile
illusion of civilian
authority across the
entire empire.
Every army legion
realized they had
the physical power
to manufacture the
world’s most powerful
ruler.
The math was
simple.
If your legion
had enough swords,
your general could
be emperor.
The throne went
to whoever promised
the troops the
largest cash bonus.
If an emperor
ran out of
money, his own
guards simply killed
him and sold
the crown again.
The result was
fifty years of
absolute chaos, known
as the Crisis
of the Third
Century.
Twenty-six different men
claimed the throne.
Almost all of
them died by
assassination.
The empire survived,
but its political
legitimacy was gone
forever, replaced by
raw military power.
That breakdown of
authority is what
you are analyzing
when you study
the collapse of
Rome.
