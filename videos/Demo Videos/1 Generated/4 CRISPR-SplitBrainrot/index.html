Bacteria fight off
killer viruses using
a tiny genetic
security system.
This natural CRISPR
system uses two
separate RNA molecules
to hunt invaders.
The first molecule
is called the
CRISPR RNA, or
crRNA.
The second partner
is the trans-activating
CRISPR RNA, or
tracrRNA.
Your crRNA molecule
holds a highly
specific twenty-nucleotide spacer
sequence.
This tiny spacer
sequence matches the
invader's viral DNA
perfectly.
The constant partner,
tracrRNA, acts as
a physical structural
scaffold.
Its looped shape
is designed to
physically grip the
Cas9 protein.
The Cas9 enzyme
is the physical
muscle that cuts
viral DNA.
Without the helper
scaffold, Cas9 cannot
grab the targeting
RNA.
These two distinct
RNA molecules must
find each other
first.
They hybridize together
to form a
functional dual-RNA guide.
This newly formed
dual-RNA complex immediately
recruits the Cas9
enzyme.
The scaffold holds
the protein while
the spacer guides
the search.
Together, they patrol
the bacterial cell
looking for matching
viral genes.
When the twenty-nucleotide
spacer finds its
match, the hunt
ends.
Cas9 unzips the
viral DNA and
slices clean through
both strands.
This split-system defense
keeps bacteria safe
from relentless viral
infections.
