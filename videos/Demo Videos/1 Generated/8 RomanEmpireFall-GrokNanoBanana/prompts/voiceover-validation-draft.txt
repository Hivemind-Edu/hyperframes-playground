Rome did not fall in one dramatic night.

It slowly forgot how to keep itself together.

Emperors changed so fast, the crown became a weapon.

Generals learned armies could make rulers, then unmake them.

Taxes rose, coins weakened, and cities began feeling abandoned.

Along the frontiers, pressure grew from hungry moving peoples.

Rome hired outsiders to defend borders it could not hold.

Some became allies; others saw power running out of answers.

In 378, Gothic fighters shattered a Roman army at Adrianople.

After that, fear started outrunning imperial orders.

In 410, Alaric entered Rome and shocked the world.

The city survived, but the myth of invincibility cracked.

Provinces broke away, taking grain, taxes, and soldiers with them.

By 476, the western emperor was easier to remove than rescue.

Constantinople endured, but in the West, Rome became a memory.

The fall was not one collapse, but a long surrender of trust.
