Rome’s biggest threat was not the barbarians at the gate.

It was the army that was supposed to defend it.

For centuries, emperors needed more than soldiers. They needed legitimacy.

A dynasty mattered. The Senate mattered. Tradition still mattered.

Then, in 235, that old system cracked open.

Emperor Severus Alexander tried buying peace on the German frontier.

His troops saw diplomacy as weakness, and weakness as betrayal.

So they murdered him in his tent and chose their own emperor.

Maximinus Thrax was not a senator. He was a soldier’s soldier.

That changed the rules. Any legion with enough swords could make a ruler.

Soon, generals were not serving emperors. They were auditioning to replace them.

The throne became a prize armies could sell for higher pay.

If an emperor ran out of money, his guards could kill him.

Rome survived the shock, but authority stopped feeling sacred.

The empire still had walls, roads, taxes, and armies.

What it lost was trust that anyone truly had the right to rule.
