Plants do not eat sunlight. They use it like a charger, loading energy into chemistry.
Inside each leaf cell, chloroplasts catch that light with chlorophyll, the green pigment that starts the reaction.
The first job is violent: light energy splits water into hydrogen, electrons, and oxygen.
That oxygen is not the plant's goal. It is leftover waste, drifting out through the leaf.
At the same time, carbon dioxide slips in through tiny pores called stomata.
Now the plant has what it needs: carbon from air, hydrogen from water, and energy from light.
Inside the chloroplast, the Calvin cycle grabs carbon atoms and locks them together step by step.
The result is glucose, a sugar molecule. It is basically stored sunlight in chemical form.
The plant can spend that glucose to grow leaves, roots, fruit, wood, and new cells.
So photosynthesis turns sunlight, water, and air into sugar, oxygen, and almost every food chain on Earth.
