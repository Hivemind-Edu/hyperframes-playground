Could you start the Industrial Revolution in ancient Rome?

Probably not.
But you are going to try.

Day one, you wake near the Forum with a modern engineering degree and incredibly suspicious Crocs.

First surprise: Rome is already an engineering machine.
Roads, aqueducts, cranes, concrete, watermills.

So you skip sightseeing and find a workshop.

Day three, you sketch a steam engine in charcoal.
The smiths understand heat, pressure, and cast metal.

But the first cylinder leaks.
The piston scrapes.
Every part is almost right, which is exactly the problem.

Week two, you need precision boring, standard screws, reliable seals, and metal that behaves the same way twice.

Rome has brilliant craftsmen.
It does not have factory tooling.

Month one, you build a Hero-style steam spinner.
It hisses.
It spins.
Everyone stares.

It also powers basically nothing.
A clever toy is not an engine room.

Month six, you try a mine pump.
For one afternoon, it works.

Then seals fail, fuel runs short, and replacement parts do not match.

Year one, a senator asks why he should buy machines when labor is cheaper.

The army wants siege engines.
Merchants want profit now.
Officials want taxes, not experiments.

You finally see the bottleneck:
not one missing invention, but an ecosystem.

Rome had engineers.
What it lacked was the world that makes engineering compound.

Inventions do not cause revolutions alone.
Supply chains do.
