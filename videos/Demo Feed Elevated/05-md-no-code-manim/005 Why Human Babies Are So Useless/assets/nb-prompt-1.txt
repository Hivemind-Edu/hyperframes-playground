GUIDELINE 1: THE TEMPLATE.
The image's foundation is defined by the following template. It sets the scene, action, and overall aesthetic.

TEMPLATE PROMPT: Iconic 3x3 storyboard in 16:9 widescreen format. Thick magenta (#FF00FF) divider lines between each panel. No text, no labels, no panel numbers. Do not draw any magenta border around the outside edge of the full image. Magenta must appear only between panels.

GUIDELINE 2: THE STYLE.
Every panel in the grid must be rendered in the following style:
Rounded stick figure illustration on pure white background, the modern simplified human form seen in Kurzgesagt character sheets and Headspace app illustrations. Characters are soft rounded blob-like bodies with smooth continuous black outlines of uniform thickness, NOT traditional line-art stick figures. Perfect circle heads with two dot eyes and a curved mouth line, smooth capsule-shaped white bodies, rounded tube arms and legs, the entire figure defined by a single clean black outline filled with flat white or very light warm grey. No shading, no gradients, no detail beyond the essential form. Emotional expression carried entirely through minimal face and body posture: tilted head for curiosity, raised arms for excitement, hunched shoulders for sadness. Props use the same clean outline style with ONE flat saturated accent color for emphasis — a red heart, an orange book, a blue phone. Pure white background with absolutely nothing else, no ground line, no shadow, no environment. One figure or object per image, never a scene. Radical simplicity creates maximum emotional clarity and educational authority.

GUIDELINE 3: THE SPECIFICS.
Panel 1: A close-up of a human newborn in a medical setting. The baby is lying on its back, arms and legs moving in disorganized, jerky flails. The skin is wrinkled, and the eyes are barely focused. A medical professional's gloved hand supports the baby's floppy head, highlighting the lack of muscular control.
Panel 2: A split-screen comparison. On the left, a newborn foal in a straw-filled stable pushes itself up on wobbly but functional legs just hours after birth. On the right, a human newborn lies prone on a soft blanket, unable to rotate its face away from the surface. The horse’s immediate mobility contrasts with the human's stillness.
Panel 3: The same split-screen scene, camera zoomed slightly closer to the foal's tensed leg muscles and the human baby's limp arm. A clinical diagram of a nervous system is faintly overlaid on both, showing dense neural connections in the foal’s motor cortex versus sparse, undeveloped pathways in the human's.
Panel 4: An anatomical study of a human skeleton from the waist down. The pelvic girdle is shown in a neutral gray. The structure is bowl-shaped and compact. A side-by-side comparison shows a chimpanzee pelvis, which is long and flat, providing a much wider and straighter exit for a birth canal.
Panel 5: A 3D anatomical diagram of the human female pelvis. Red arrows indicate the mechanical pressures of bipedalism: weight bearing down from the spine and up from the femurs. The birth canal is highlighted, showing its constricted, oval shape that requires a fetus to rotate to pass through.
Panel 6: The same 3D anatomical diagram, camera tilted upward to include a human skull above the pelvis. The skull is highlighted in a glowing amber color, signifying metabolic energy. The brain inside is large and complex, occupying a significant portion of the cranial volume. The scale of the head relative to the pelvic opening is clear.
Panel 7: A cross-section diagram showing the 'obstetrical dilemma.' A full-term fetal head is positioned at the pelvic inlet. The fit is extremely tight, with only millimeters of clearance. The skull plates are shown as separate, unfused bones designed to compress during the transit through the narrow bony ring.
Panel 8: An evolutionary timeline showing the gestation length of various primates. A red marker indicates the point where the human fetal head size would exceed the pelvic opening. A shaded area shows the 'safety zone' where birth must occur before the brain grows too large for the mother's survival.
Panel 9: The same evolutionary timeline, camera zoomed in on the human 'birth' point. Icons of motor development — crawling, grasping, standing — are all positioned months to the right of the birth line. The human infant is born in a state of 'extrauterine gestation,' essentially a fourth trimester outside the womb.
