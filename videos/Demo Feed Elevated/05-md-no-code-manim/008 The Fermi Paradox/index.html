The Fermi Paradox
describes a simple
contradiction.
If life is
possible on Earth,
it should be
possible on many
other planets.
The Milky Way
has hundreds of
billions of stars.
Many have planets.
Some are rocky.
Some sit in
stable orbits.
And the galaxy
is old, much
older than human
civilization.
If intelligent life
happens often, some
civilizations should be
millions of years
ahead of us.
A million-year head
start is enough
to send probes,
signals, or machines
across huge distances.
They do not
need faster-than-light travel.
Slow expansion is
enough over galactic
time.
So we should
expect evidence somewhere:
a signal, a
probe, a changed
star, a megastructure,
something.
But so far,
there is no
confirmed signal from
another civilization.
No confirmed alien
probe.
No confirmed megastructure.
That is the
paradox: the numbers
make the galaxy
look crowded, but
the evidence says
silence.
So one assumption
is wrong.
Maybe life is
extremely rare.
Maybe simple life
is common, but
intelligence is rare.
Maybe technological civilizations
destroy themselves, go
quiet, or leave
traces we do
not recognize.
Or maybe our
search is still
too small.
The Fermi Paradox
is not proof
that we are
alone.
It is proof
that one of
our easy guesses
about life in
the galaxy is
probably wrong.
